The Exposition of the Ganapatya Doctrine

The sages, led by the venerable Sanaka, had listened to the Guru's words with rapt attention. Yet a deeper curiosity stirred within them. They bowed once more and spoke.

The sages Sanaka and others said:
"O revered teacher, you have revealed the truths of so many paths with such clarity. Now, out of your boundless compassion, kindly explain to us the philosophical doctrine of the Ganapatyas. What is the nature of their understanding? How do they perceive the Supreme?"
The great Ganaka, the Guru of Gurus, smiled. His eyes shone with the light of transcendental wisdom as he prepared to unveil the heart of the Ganapatya vision.

The Ganaka said:
"Listen, O wise ones, to the doctrine of Ganesha as remembered in the Vedas. It is spoken of in two ways, both united under the term 'Ganeshadvaita'—the non-dual truth of Ganesha. He who is proclaimed as the witness of all, the silent consciousness that observes every phenomenon, is endowed with the union of pure consciousness and its creative power, Maya. That very being is known as Ganaraja, the King of all Ganas, and is spoken of as the Fifth Principle, the Panchama.

Though united with Maya for the purpose of creation and divine play, He transcends and overcomes all limiting adjuncts that Maya imposes. He is truly without any limitation, of the very nature of the attributeless Self. Yet the Ganapatyas declare Him to be both Nirguna, without attributes, and Saguna, with attributes, for He contains all possibilities within His infinite being. The Ganapatyas firmly accept nothing superior to this Fifth Principle. For them, He is the ultimate, the final reality beyond which nothing exists. They understand that even Ishvara, the personal Lord, undergoes dissolution and creation within Him. The cycles of cosmic manifestation and withdrawal occur within the vast expanse of His being.

Maya, which manifests as the fourfold universe—the gross, subtle, causal, and transcendental realms—attains repose in the Fifth Principle during the great dissolution. When creation arises again, Maya emerges from Him just as before, like waves rising from the ocean. She abides in the Fifth Principle as both distinct and non-distinct, separate yet inseparable, like the power of fire to burn which is both one with fire and yet conceptually distinct. Thus proclaim the followers of Ganesha, grounded firmly in the Vedic path. Now I shall explain to you the deeper doctrine of the path of Ganeshadvaita.
In the beginningless state, beyond time and space, abides Brahman, established in Its own supreme glory. For the sake of divine play, through Its own inherent power, that Brahman became twofold. From this primal state emerged Mahamaya, the great creative power, in the form of Bindu, the primordial point. She is spoken of as Mula Prakriti, the original nature.

Mahamaya holds the three Gunas—Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas—in perfect equilibrium, abiding without any limitation. She is of all forms, containing every possibility within her womb. The Purusha, the conscious principle, is formless, the silent witness. Taking refuge in the Purusha, she becomes the supreme Prakriti, the active creative power.

Both Prakriti and Purusha arise from Brahman. Just as from the sun arises its reflection in water, and another reflection in space, so too do these manifestations arise from the one Supreme. The reflected sun appears different yet is essentially one with the original.

Brahman united with Prakriti is remembered as Saguna, the Lord with attributes. When the condition of Saguna ceases, through the process of dissolution, Brahman returns to its state of Nirguna, the attributeless. Even this state of Nirguna, according to the scriptures, ultimately merges back into the Supreme, beyond all conceptualization. Both the state of having attributes and the state of being without attributes are conceptions within Brahman itself. Through these dual states, the greatness of the Supreme is described in the Vedas and Upanishads. Yet Brahman itself transcends both.

From the Saguna Lord arise the universe, the individual soul, and Ishvara, the personal God. For one who has fallen into the world of duality, who is bound by the limitations of Maya, the earth itself becomes the support. Just as a fallen man takes support of the earth to rise again, so too does the bound soul need a support.

For one bound by Maya, liberation from Maya's bondage is attained by taking refuge in Brahman that is united with Maya—the Saguna Lord who is both transcendent and immanent. This is the path of devotion, of worship, of relationship with the Divine.

By reliance upon the Fifth Principle, upon Ganesha himself, the Maya that forms the fourfold universe perishes. The bonds of illusion are broken. By reliance upon the Nirguna, the attributeless, the Fifth itself dissolves into the Supreme. When the root Maya is destroyed, when the very seed of illusion is burned in the fire of knowledge, Brahmanhood is attained.

Thus the followers of Ganeshadvaita declare Brahman to be threefold: the Nirguna, the Saguna, and the Turiya which is both and neither. Yet their final doctrine, their ultimate realization, is Supreme self-bliss—ananda—beyond all categories. Of this there is no doubt.

That Brahman, that Supreme Reality, is spoken of only by the name of Ganesha, and by no other name in its fullness. The name contains the essence, the form embodies the formless.

According to Vedic authority, there is no ultimate difference between the two modes of worship—the worship of the Saguna and the meditation on the Nirguna. Both lead to the same goal. The exalted Fifth State, the Panchama, is said to be the abode of supreme bliss, the Chintamani-dvipa, the island of wish-fulfilling gems, where all desires are fulfilled in the bliss of the Self.

The Fourth State, the Turiya, is indeed spoken of as this Chintamani-dvipa, the divine realm of Ganesha. Maha-Buddhi, the great intelligence, is remembered as consisting of the five modifications of the mind—the very fabric of our cognitive experience. Siddhi, the power of accomplishment, is so called because she grants both joy and sorrow to the mind, guiding the soul through its journey.

Siddhi and Buddhi are Mahamaya herself, the great creative power, manifesting as the devoted consorts of Ganesha. They are not separate from Him but are His own powers, His Shakti, through which He creates, sustains, and dissolves the universe.

The Fifth-ness of Ganesha, His status as the Panchama, is fully accepted in pure non-dualism. His lordship over all and His identity as Brahman are affirmed in the revealed scriptures. He is both the personal Lord and the impersonal Absolute, the source and the destination, the beginning and the end.

Thus, O sages, is the Ganapatya doctrine—a path of supreme devotion and profound knowledge, leading the sincere seeker from the worship of forms to the realization of the formless, from the many to the One, from bondage to liberation, all through the grace of the elephant-faced Lord who is none other than the Supreme Brahman itself."

A profound silence enveloped the assembly as the sages absorbed this wisdom. The teachings of all paths now appeared as facets of a single jewel, and at the heart of that jewel shone the form of Ganesha, the Lord of Beginnings, the Remover of Obstacles, the Supreme Reality.
Thus concludes the Exposition of the Ganapatya Doctrine.
