The Description of Jambudvipa

The sages, led by the venerable Bhrigu, bowed in reverence and posed their question with deep curiosity.

The Sages like Bhrigu said:
“O Lord, the knowers of the Vedas declare Jambudvipa to be the sacred land of action, Karmabhumi. Therefore, O Lotus-born One, please describe to us its true and exact configuration.”

A serene smile graced the lips of the creator as he prepared to unveil the cosmic geography.

Brahma said:

“Hear now, O great sages, of Jambudvipa, the landmass known by that name, situated in the very middle of the vast ocean of salt. It is established in the sky and air, its form resembling that of a hen's egg.

This, O great sages, is the sphere of the element earth, composed of the five fundamental elements. This great island is divided into nine distinct parts. Its northern part is considered the head-region, and the southern part is said to be the foot-region.

These nine regions are: Kuru, Ramyaka, and the region known as Hiranyavarsha. Upon the back, the very spine of the Earth-goddess, are situated Bharata, Kimpurusha, Harivarsha, and Ilavrita. To the eastern side, O Brahmins, are situated Bhadrashva and Ketumala. Thus, the parts of the Earth-goddess have been described to you.

Envision the Earth, Kashyapi, in a divine posture. She has lifted her right arm, which is the southern part, and placed her head downwards. She stands, having formed her waist with the palm of her left hand.

The region above her neck is said to be her face, which is the land of Ilavrita. Her chest is the part of Harivarsha, and her abdomen is remembered as Bharata. The region between her chest and abdomen is the varsha known as Kimpurusha.

From her left shoulder, extending all the way to the end of her left foot, is the part known as Bhadrashva. On her right side, starting from the hip region down to the foot, is remembered as Ketumala. The head-region, covered with hair, is the varsha known as Ramyaka.

The region of the hip and the back, the area above the anus, is proclaimed as Kuru-varsha, situated on the rear half, O best of sages. The upper half of this area is said to be the part of Hiranyavarsha. And for each single varsha, two large subsidiary islands are also remembered.

In times past, the Danava king, Shurapad, lived in the southern part of Bhadrashva. By the command of Skamba, the ocean, many Danavas resided on that island. Wherever these wicked ones settled, they devoured and corrupted that part of the land. Because of this, Bhadrashva-varsha became fragmented, taking on the form of various islands. Other such parts also came into being, and they exist in a certain sequence. In all of these lands, the territories are divided into five categories, O Brahmins!

In regions suitable for penance—in the high mountains and in the deep forests—the great sages made their abode. In the regions meant for enjoyment, the best of celestials resided. Celestial beings like the Gandharvas, and humans with their distinct social orders, lived happily on lands deemed suitable for action.

In regions meant for both enjoyment and action, lived the various inhabitants of the lands of pleasure: mortals, Gandharvas, and others like them, in all places suited to their nature. Those who had fallen from the righteous duties of Varna and Ashrama lived there under the rule of kings. The Gandharvas and other such beings, endowed with divine powers, are thus described. The sages, however, endowed with immense yogic power, are considered superior even to them.

There are those who possess a nature that is both mortal and divine, born from the groups of humans. Some of these beings are perceptible to the senses, while others are imperceptible, O great sages! The groups of deities can remain at will, unseen by the eyes of mortals. The wicked among mortals could not see them, for they were imperceptible.

The teachers of the three groups starting with the Gandharvas, O best of sages, as well as the Danavas and Rakshasas, all originally sprang from the race of the Devas themselves. Throughout the cycles of the Krita, Treta, Dvapara, and Kali yugas, and the periods that lie between them, these beings, having attained a position of dominance, would subdue the Devas, sages, and humans by their own power. Those who revelled in sensual pleasures would then rule over all the lands for the benefit of their own races.

Yet, in other periods, the sages, the groups of Devas, and righteous humans would reside, full of happy enjoyments, mutually engaged in each other's welfare. Among the groups of divine beings, the Danavas, Rakshasas, Bhutas, Pretas, Pishachas, and other lowly ones are proclaimed. Those mortals, Bhutas, Pishachas, Pretas, Daityas, and Rakshasas who dwell in the lands of enjoyment are also declared as the lowly ones. So too are those among mortals who are devoid of the duties of Varna and Ashrama, those who follow heretical paths, and the non-Aryas.

In the Kali Yuga, these lowly mortals, attaining the status of the superior ones through their own prowess and with the aid of the age itself, subdue the humans who adhere to the duties of Varna and Ashrama. They then live happily, enjoying sensual pleasures for the benefit of their own race.

Now hear of the languages of these lands. The language of the immortals—the Devas and the sages—is proclaimed as Deva-bhasha. The languages of the various groups of divine beings are many, each distinct from the other. For the inhabitants of the lands of enjoyment, eighteen different languages are remembered. In the Krita, Treta, and Dvapara ages, the lowly humans on earth—those on islands at the very edge of the ocean, on lands of sand and copper, in places unsuitable for residence by karmic mortals—were established by the first kings. These were the lowly Mleccha humans. They resided in various places, always few in number.

In order to conduct their affairs, these base men created for themselves distinct and separate languages. At the time of the incarnation of the sage Vyasa, a hundred Mleccha languages are remembered. Even now, new Mleccha languages are continually born upon the earth. The languages that are born also perish, O Brahmins! The Mleccha languages are transient. The language of the celestial beings in this world, however, was well-refined by Lord Indra himself.

Those born from the mouth, the Brahmins, would speak the refined Sanskrit. Those born from the feet, the Shudras, would speak the unrefined Prakrit, which is like a shadow of the celestial tongue. Women, too, would speak among those endowed with Varna and Ashrama. Thus, the Deva-bhasha is of two kinds: Sanskrit and Prakrit. This dual distinction is declared in the very form of the Vedas and other scriptures. The languages of the groups of divine beings are said to be seventeen in number. They reside, having taken refuge in the shadow of the celestial language. But for the inhabitants of the lands of enjoyment, the languages take on different and distinct forms.

All these beings, in their own ways, are ultimately followers of the path of the Vedas on earth, without a doubt. Thus, the nature of the languages has been described to you. And know this: the teachings that explain the conclusions of the sixty-four philosophical systems were first spoken in the language of the celestial beings by the great sages themselves.
