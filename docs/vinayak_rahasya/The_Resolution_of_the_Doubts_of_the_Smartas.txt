The Resolution of the Doubts of the Smärtas

The Guru, accompanied by his disciples, journeyed to the sacred land of Brahmävarta. There, after performing the worship of Vighnaraja, the Lord of Obstacles, with full devotion, the Guru—who was himself the teacher of their own Guru—took up residence. The people of that region, Brahmins and other devout followers of the Vedic path, came to meet him. Bowing with deep reverence, they spoke from hearts full of devotion.

The People said:
“Revered Sir, we have been instructed here by the great yogi Samvarta, the son of Angiras, a sage who is firmly established in the realization of Brahman.

We are known as Smärtas, those who follow the middle path, honouring the five great deities: Ganesha, Shambhu, Govinda, Bhuvanādini (the Goddess), and Surya. We understand these five deities of the Panchäyatana to be manifestations of the one Fourth Brahman, the Turiya. They are like the five limbs of a single body; there is no ultimate distinction between them.

Our practice is to establish at the center the deity towards whom one feels the deepest devotion, placing the others respectfully in the surrounding directions, and to worship them all in sequence. These deities, though appearing diverse through the play of the three Gunas, perform their divine pastimes in the world, interacting with one another. By their own free will and perfect resolve, they assume various relationships—of father and son, or of worshipper and worshipped.

According to the authority of the Vedas and Shastras, there is no inherent inferiority or superiority among them. To worship them collectively is to worship the complete, undivided Brahman. O Guru, we have no doubt that to worship any one of them, as they are all forms imbued with the Gunas of Brahman, is to perform the worship of the whole. Indeed, worshipping a single limb is verily the worship of the Turiya. These Panchäyatana deities are all forms of the one Ishvara.

We understand the cosmic form thus: from the face of the Turiya emerged Shakti, constituting His left side. Surya, constituting His right side, manifests from the throat downwards. Mahavishnu extends to the midpoint of the eyebrows, and Sadashiva to their end. Ganeshvara, the head, is the point of integration for all four. Thus, among the five parts, the head is paramount. The deity who is that head is Vighnesha, and for this reason, He has attained the status of the first-worshipped, by the direct injunction of the Vedas.
This is our understanding, established in the Vedic scriptures. However, the followers of the Panchama path, the Ganapatyas, proclaim a different view. While we see our chosen deity as the Turiya and the others as manifestations of His qualities, they proclaim Ganesha as the Fifth, the Supreme Lord, and declare Him to be the very cause of the Turiya, citing scriptural evidence. A great doubt has therefore arisen in our minds. O Teacher, we bow to you. Please tell us the truth of this conclusion.” The assembly fell silent, awaiting the wisdom of the sage Ganaka. He acknowledged their sincere query with a gentle nod and began to elucidate the profound truth.

The Ganaka said:
“The Supreme Brahman, inherently self-aware, engaged in the divine play of creation. From its state of pure unity, it manifested a twofold nature. One aspect is the attributeless, Nirguna Brahman—pure consciousness alone. The other is of the nature of Maya, which is illusory and of the nature of delusion.

This eternal Maya depends upon the Nirguna Brahman for its existence. A portion of that Nirguna Brahman thus became associated with Maya. This portion, united with Maya, is of the nature of Chinmaya—the union of Consciousness and Maya. Within this, the Bindu, representing Saguna Brahman, became established as an independent principle. Thus, Brahman is one, yet appears twofold: one with Maya, and the other utterly free from it. From this distinction, all of creation arose. The aspect connected with Maya is Saguna; the aspect free from it is Nirguna. The Bindu form is Saguna, of the nature of full, radiant effulgence.

This state of Chinmaya is proclaimed in the eternal scriptures as the abode of the tranquility of the three gunas, inherently free from limitations. The great Maya residing within it is called the Fifth, Panchami, whose power is twofold. Buddhi, the intellectual principle, constitutes the right side, while Siddhi, the principle of accomplishment, constitutes the left. Buddhi is said to be material and activates effort; the root form is Mahasiddhi, and Buddhi is also called Devi. Buddhi pervades all, while Siddhi bestows the delight in that all-pervasion. Siddhi resides below the throat, and Buddhi in the head. When these two exist in a state of non-difference, it is called Saguna Brahman.
This Ganapati Brahman is proclaimed as the Fifth. From this very entity, by His will, a Maya consisting of pure Sattva emerged, taking the form of the Fourth, Turiya. United with that, Brahman appeared as Ishvara, who is known as the Fourth, the cause of all worlds. 
From Ishvara Himself originated Avidya, ignorance, which gives rise to the sense of individuality. The individual soul is that consciousness, limited by Maya and existing in dependence upon Ishvara.
Thus, Maya has the form of the Fourth, and the reflection within it is the individual soul. The four deities—Shiva, Vishnu, Devi, Surya—are remembered as being united with this Maya. Their essential, true Self, however, is the Fifth, Ganapati. There is no doubt that the Fifth is also united with Maya, while the four with the form of Turiya are remembered as having bodily forms. Due to the non-difference of the body and the Self, they speak of five within the Turiya. The Fourth, Guneshvara, is the synthesis of the four, which are distinguished based on differences in wisdom or aspect.
Therefore, the state of being a Guna-manifestation and the Fourth is proclaimed for Shiva and others. For Ganesha, the state of being both the Fifth and the Fourth is proclaimed. Hence, O best of Brahmins, there is no fault in any of these views. They are all valid perspectives on the one Truth.

In the Vaishnava texts, Shiva is remembered as the son of Brahma; in the Shaiva texts, Vishnu and Brahma are described as possessing Gunas and being dependent forms; and in the Ganapatya texts, Ganesha is described as the Supreme Self, both the Fifth and the Fourth. The Trinity are often described as endowed with one predominant Guna, while the Supreme Shakti and Surya are remembered as possessing all three. In some places, all four are remembered as having the status of Turiya. The Vedic Upanishads themselves establish the true principle of each respective deity.
Brahman itself is the ultimate deity of the Smärta path. In the relative realm of Saguna, it is remembered in the Vedas as fivefold. Whether one envisions the qualified Fourth or the supreme Fifth, among the Panchäyatana deities, Ganeshvara holds primacy and is to be worshipped first by all, according to Vedic authority—this is true and without doubt.

The five are but the qualified forms of the attributeless Brahman. The nature of each deity is to be understood from the scriptures that elucidate each one. O Brahmins! One should understand through the respective, authoritative scriptures of each tradition. To judge one tradition by the texts of another leads only to superficial and confused knowledge. Followers of the Smärta path should, therefore, study the scriptures of all five traditions. Through this comprehensive study, knowledge becomes harmonious, unified, and complete.”

A profound sense of clarity and peace descended upon the Smärtas. Their doubts were resolved, replaced by a deeper, more inclusive understanding. Hearing these conclusive words, they bowed with great reverence to the Guru and departed, their hearts filled with joy and unity.
The Guru then proceeded to the hermitage of Samvarta. After worshipping Lord Ganesha, he sat down and instructed the sage, “Those who reside in Brahmävarta—give them the authentic Vedic texts and instruct them properly in this essential Truth.”

Hearing the command of his Guru, Samvarta replied, “I will do so exactly as you have said.” Bowing respectfully to his teacher, the great sage Samvarta then departed to carry out this sacred task.
