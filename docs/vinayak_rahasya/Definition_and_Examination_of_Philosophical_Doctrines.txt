Definition and Examination of Philosophical Doctrines

Sanaka and the other sages said:

O best of spiritual guides, please gracefully explain to us the true nature and definition of a doctrinal path.

Ganaka said:

To attain the state of Brahman and to cross over the ocean of worldly existence, an incomparable path was conceptualized by the ancient Yogis who were deeply established in that supreme divine state. That which is accepted by many sages is called a Mata. Yogis use terms like Mata, Marga, and Samaya interchangeably to describe it.

That doctrinal path is remembered as two-fold based on the distinction between Arya and Anarya. Seeking salvation through non-Vedic paths is like trying to cross a bottomless ocean using nothing but one's bare arms. Such is the nature of the Anarya path described; true liberation cannot be achieved through it. Conversely, the Arya path is remembered as crossing the ocean safely via a sturdy boat.

The Arya doctrine is defined by its compliance with the duties and code of conduct of Varna and Ashrama. Its structural authorities are Shruti, Smriti, Mimamsa, Yoga, and Tantra. These five fields of knowledge serve as the authentic validation for the Arya tradition. Furthermore, the Arya doctrine itself is split into two methodologies: Tantrika and Vaidika.

The Vedas are also considered two-fold, classified into Para and Apara. The Upanishads constitute the Para, while the core collections of hymns are known as Apara. The Apara Veda consists of praises and liturgical rituals, whereas the Para directly awakens the experiential knowledge of ultimate reality. Similarly, Smriti texts are split into two categories based on Karma and Jnana. The Karma-smriti consists of the legal and ethical codes composed by sages like Manu. On the other hand, the Epics and Histories are remembered as Jnana-smriti.

Mimamsa philosophy is also stated to be two-fold based on the distinction between Dharma and Brahman. The former establishes the definitive meaning and execution of the Apara Vedas. The investigation into the lower Veda is named Purva-Mimamsa, whereas the inquiry into the higher Veda is called Uttara-Mimamsa. Together with their branches, Shruti, Smriti, and Mimamsa form the fourteen classical systems of knowledge.

Standing outside these fourteen traditional branches are Yoga and Tantra-shastra. Among all, Shruti alone is the absolute, self-evident authority, as it directly emerged from the mouth of Ishvara. O lords of sages, the science of Smriti exists solely to elaborate and expand upon the core meanings of Shruti. The path arising directly out of the harmonious alignment of Shruti and Smriti is termed the true Vaidika path.

Those sectarian scriptures, such as the Agamas and others that stand completely outside the fourteen great orthodox systems of knowledge, are not to be independently accepted by strict followers of the Vedic path. The scripture that bestows the authentic path of Knowledge and Yoga is considered inherently contained within Mimamsa. For alternative or external texts, only those portions that do not contradict the Vedas are acceptable to Vedic practitioners.

Tantra-shastra is proclaimed to have four distinct internal divisions: Vaidika, Mishra, Pashanda, and Tantrika. Based on the authority of Shruti and Smriti, only that Tantra-shastra which strictly conforms to the Vedas is acceptable to the best of Vedic scholars; all other non-conforming variations must be rejected.

Samkhya philosophy is also remembered as two-fold: Nirishvara and Seshvara. Within theistic Samkhya, only those aspects affirmed by Shruti are to be accepted by the wise. Other aspects must be abandoned. The atheistic Samkhya is completely censurable and condemned by the Vedas. Any section of any text that directly opposes Shruti must always be rejected by Vedic practitioners.

O Brahmins, in texts like the Tantras, the true structural meanings of the Shrutis have often been distorted via deviant interpretations simply to delude and mystify common people. Therefore, the inner meaning of the Shruti should never be accepted by Vedic people through the interpretive lens of the Tantric path; it must only be understood in total conformity with Smriti. There is no doubt about this.

Practices such as wearing a Lingam around the neck or branding the body with hot seals are claimed in those Tantras to be based on Shruti, but this is asserted merely to trick and mislead people. These Tantras construct highly artificial, alternative interpretations of the Shruti. O excellent ones, even some Puranas and certain texts posing as ethical codes have been written by certain authors under the heavy influence of Tantra. Such books must be entirely abandoned by the leaders of Brahmins who are dedicated to pristine Vedic conduct.

Those who preach distorted meanings of Shruti and Smriti are decried as abusers of the Veda. The same applies to those literalists who cherry-pick and accept only an isolated part of the Shruti as absolute proof. Even if they perform outward Vedic rituals, such single-text fundamentalists are equal to Tantric practitioners. All those who bind themselves exclusively to one manual are condemned by the Veda.

Those who exclusively follow a single isolated philosophical tract—be it only Mimamsa, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, or the devotion aphorisms of Shandilya—are truly censured by Vedic stalwarts and are not considered genuine followers of the Vaidika path. Without a doubt, they are as censurable as the exclusive sectarian followers of the Pancaratra path. Advaita, Vaidika, and Smarta are simply different traditional names for the self-same true Vedic path.

That which is explicitly taught in the Upanishads is declared as Advaita. That which is set forth in the other sections of the Vedas is called Vaidika. That which is aligned with the texts of righteousness, Epics, and Histories is called Smarta. O twice-born ones, the supreme doctrine unified across the Vedas, Upanishads, and Smritis is alone proclaimed as Vaidika, alone called Smarta, and is declared as Advaita by those who have crossed to the further shores of Vedic wisdom.

The supreme divinity of this Advaita path is proclaimed to be Param Brahman. That single, undivided Brahman is described in five ways in the Vedas based on its structural states or functional positions. I shall explain that to you; listen with rapt attention.

Brahman alone, established in its native, absolute majesty, serves as the immutable substratum of Maya. The supreme reality manifesting as the seamless union of Maya and Brahman is called Saguna. When completely independent and devoid of that Maya, it is termed Nirguna. O lords of sages, Brahman is thus described as two-fold strictly for the purpose of empirical dealings. From that very Saguna source, Maya manifested an overflow of pure clarity.

A reflection of the Supreme Self, Brahman, fell upon that Sattva. Within that Sattva-dominant Maya, there arose the cosmic reflection of Brahman. The absolute consciousness conditioned by Maya, along with its cosmic reflection, is named Ishvara by those who have mastered the scriptures of Vedanta. Thereafter, when mixed with impulses, that Maya became dominated by contaminated Sattva, which generates individuality. It is then named Avidya, and that which is conditioned by this Avidya, along with the individual reflection caught within it, is termed Kshetrajna. Subsequently, when heavily dominated by inertia, that same Maya is known as Prakriti.

It is the singular, non-dual Brahman alone that appears to have assumed these distinct operational forms. In the Vedas, those who focus exclusively on the Kshetrajna aspect are celebrated as the Sauras. Those who focus on the qualified, majestic, all-pervading Ishvara are remembered as the Vaishnavas. Those who worship Ishvara as specifically conditioned by pristine cosmic Maya are recorded as the Shaivas. Those who proclaim that the eternal Saguna Brahman—which is the seamless union of Pure Consciousness and Maya—is the ultimate cause of everything, are recognized in the Vedas as the Ganapatyas.

Those who sing of Maya, existing in the mere proximity and power of Brahman, as the primary source of cosmic creation are called the Mahashaktas. Conversely, those who trace everything back to the unconditioned Nirguna are designated as the Smartas by the finest compilers of Vedic scriptures. In this precise manner, the ultimate cause of the universes, Brahman, is spoken of in a six-fold way.

The unconditioned backdrop transcending Kshetrajna, Ishvara, and Maya is declared to be the Fourth State. The state corresponding to the seamless union of Consciousness and Maya is sung as the Fifth State. Master Yogis declare the state of the ultimate Nirguna Brahman to be Turiyatita. I shall now explain the core doctrines of all these six paths; listen with due respect.

Thus ends the Definition and Examination of Philosophical Doctrines.
