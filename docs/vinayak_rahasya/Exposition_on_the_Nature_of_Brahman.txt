Exposition on the Nature of Brahman

Sanaka and the other sages said:

O revered teacher, please explain to us the true nature of Brahman and also its defining characteristics.

Ganaka said:

"That Thou Art" transcends the threefold division of words and meanings and reveals one's own supreme majesty. It is beyond the reach of mind and speech and is supreme, filled with the peace of Yoga. That Brahman, described in the Shruti as Bhuma, the Infinite, is indescribable. The statement "Tat Tvam Asi" is the supreme form through superimposition used for realization. Vainayaki, the great Maya, whose nature is self-luminous awareness, assumed differentiated forms only for the proper accomplishment of superimposed teaching. In the Shruti she is spoken of in two ways: as the giver of delusion and as the holder of delusion. Mahasiddhi is regarded as the giver of delusion, while Buddhi is considered its bearer. Brahman, present within Siddhi and Buddhi, is declared to be directly the Self. 

In the Vedas that employ superimposition, it is spoken of as a supporting post. Buddhi is remembered as its head, and Siddhi as its embodied form. This self-aware essence is called Brahman of intrinsic bliss. Where a defined object is intended, this is described as a support-post. From such definable reference arises Maya in two forms: Vidya and Avidya. Vidya is of the nature of intrinsic bliss and of existence-consciousness-bliss. In the Vedas and allied texts, it is described as Maya without limiting adjuncts. The reflection of Brahman within Vidya is pure and remembered as attributeless. That is the Supreme Self, the Supreme Brahman; Ganesha is that supreme Nirguna reality. Such names are conceived by Yogins for the sake of understanding. Avidya, associated with limiting adjuncts, takes the form of Bindu and Pranava. 

United with the four divisions of the universe, it is regarded as the fifth state. That supreme Bindu, endowed with conditioned Maya, is beyond the beyond. Brahman united with the full syllable Om is Saguna and of the nature of Pranava. The Saguna Purusha is the individual soul, the human being of empirical reality. Such designations are conceived by Yogins. Brahman associated with Tvam is Saguna, while that of Tat is Nirguna. That which is associated with duality and that which is free from duality is declared to be the Asi form—Brahman known as Svananda. 

That which is associated with triple states and that which transcends them is remembered as Brahman in the form of Bhuma, filled with yogic peace. Thus, Brahman is explained as fourfold in the Vedas and related texts. It is majestic, infinite in nature, and free from all transactional activity. Through both Saguna and Nirguna aspects, the supreme is called Svananda. The Asi form of Brahman is regarded as real and as engaging in manifold play. Associated with cosmic play, it is Saguna and of the nature of Pranava. But the Nirguna Supreme Brahman is the illuminator of that play itself. 

Through both attributes and attribute-less states, Svananda is known as the Divine Player. That which has limiting adjuncts is grasped everywhere in the Shruti as possessing qualities. In practical dealings and in the Vedas, the Nirguna is not emphasized in the same way. The fourfold creation of the universe arises only from the fifth state, and not otherwise. Thus the fourfold Brahman is described in the Nigamas. That Supreme Purusha sustains countless crores of universes. Some declare Brahman to be unmanifest and of universal form. According to their view, Brahman is clearly explained as fivefold. Some speak of it as threefold, differentiated as Tat, Om, and Sat. This is the doctrine of the Ganesha tradition, in accordance with the path of the Tapini Upanishad.

Thus ends the Exposition on the Nature of Brahman.
